why I built it

what it spares you

A mouse keeps the hand gripping, the wrist bent and the arm reaching out to the side. With the arrow keys the hands stay where they type. Here is roughly what an ordinary week of browsing with the extension spares your body.

The week is an example, not anyone’s data: five working days, opening about 80 links and pressing about 400 arrows a day. The extension’s stats page shows the same figure for your own browsing, counted on your computer and never sent anywhere.

A mock-up, not the extension — but the ring is the real one. Click a row, then press ↓ ↑ ← →.

keys

↓ ↑
Move to the element above or below.
← →
Move across the row you are on.
Home End
Jump to either end of the line you are on. The last arrow you pressed decides what a line means: after moving sideways it is the row, after moving up or down it is the column.
Enter
Activate the focused element. On a text box or dropdown, it hands focus over so you can type or pick. On a list row it activates the link inside the row, since that is where the row keeps its href.
Esc
Leave a text box and go back to navigating the page.
Esc again
Hand the keyboard back to the site, so its own shortcuts work — space to play a video, or anything else it binds. Press it once more to carry on navigating.
F10
Open the menu — duplicate the page, go back, select text, report a problem, or see every key. Esc closes it.

how it works

The extension reads the page, works out which elements are worth stopping on, and treats them as a grid. Arrow keys move between them by position rather than by DOM order, so moving right goes to the thing on the right, not to whatever happens to come next in the markup.

Keystrokes are intercepted before the page sees them, so sites with their own arrow-key handlers do not fight you for control. Text boxes, dropdowns and anything else that needs arrow keys of its own are left alone until you hand focus over with Enter.

The toolbar popup keeps a running count of the actions you have taken and links to a stats page. When something goes wrong, F10 → Report a problem shows you a short log of what the extension did on the page, and sends it with your description to the developer. Nothing is sent until you press Send report; see privacy.
